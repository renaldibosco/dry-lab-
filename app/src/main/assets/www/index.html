1. Your DNA sequence

Paste DNA, or open a FASTA (.fasta, .fa), GenBank (.gb) or text file. Numbers and spaces are removed for you. RNA (U) is changed to DNA (T).

G + C (3 bonds, holds tighter) A + T (2 bonds)

GC content matters for heat: G-C pairs have 3 hydrogen bonds, A-T pairs have 2. More GC means the DNA needs more heat to separate (a higher melting temperature).

GC along the sequence

Peaks above the red line (65%) are GC-rich spots. They are harder to copy by PCR and may need 98 °C, DMSO or betaine.

2. Genes: start and stop codons

An open reading frame (ORF) starts at a start codon (ATG) and runs in steps of 3 bases until a stop codon (TAA, TAG or TGA). Genes can be on either strand, so both are searched.

Analyze a sequence first.

Arrows on top read left to right (+ strand). Arrows below read right to left (- strand).

3. PCR primers

Primers are short DNA pieces (18-25 bases) that mark where copying starts. Good primers have Tm 55-65 °C, GC 40-60%, one or two G/C at the 3' end, and do not stick to themselves.

Settings (primer rules and reaction salts)

Cloning add-on: restriction sites on the primer ends

Adds an enzyme site plus 4 extra bases (GCGC) to the 5' end, so the PCR product can be cut and pasted into a plasmid. The extra bases do not change the binding Tm shown.

4. PCR program: temperatures and times

Each cycle has three temperatures: very hot to separate the DNA strands (denaturation), cooler so primers can stick (annealing), then 72 °C where the polymerase copies (extension). Annealing temperature (Ta) comes from the primers' Tm.

Master mix calculator

Make one mix for all tubes plus a little extra (pipetting loses some), then add template DNA to each tube. Always include a no-template control (water instead of DNA).
